\section{Idea}

\citet{kashyap2025} have one cash bond and one future with identical exposure, so the basis trade is a perfect hedge and the basis pays for balance sheet only. We add a factor and a bond. Returns load on a level and a slope. Bond 1 and the future have the same loadings. Bond 2 has the same level loading and a larger slope loading. A fund long bond 2 and short the future hedges the level and keeps the slope. Its excess return is the basis plus the slope premium. The slope realisation is the risk.

In the data bond 2 is the bond funds actually hold, which sits further out on the curve than the cheapest to deliver of the contract it would be hedged with. Matching dollar duration with a shorter CTD leaves the level hedged and the slope open.

\section{Original model}

Short rate $r$, bond yield $r_T$, futures implied yield $r_F = r_T - x$. The basis $x$ is the excess return of long bond, short future. Dealers and hedge funds are risk neutral and level neutral. Asset managers hold duration by mean variance and a fixed fraction $\theta$ of it in futures. Hedge fund demand for the trade, with equity $E_H$, capital per unit $\gamma$ and alternative returns $c - \beta X_H / 2$, is
\begin{equation}
D_H = \frac{E_H}{\gamma} - \frac{c}{\beta \gamma} + \frac{x}{\beta \gamma^2} ,
\label{eq:dh}
\end{equation}
and dealer demand is $D_D = K_D - a/b + x/(b\phi)$. Futures clearing gives $x$.

\section{Two factors, three assets}

Shocks $\varepsilon_L$ and $\varepsilon_S$, mean zero, variances $\sigma_L^2$ and $\sigma_S^2$, uncorrelated. Excess returns
\begin{align}
R_1 &= \pi_1 - \ell \varepsilon_L - s \varepsilon_S , \\
R_2 &= \pi_2 - \ell \varepsilon_L - (s + \Delta) \varepsilon_S , \\
R_F &= \pi_F - \ell \varepsilon_L - s \varepsilon_S , \qquad \pi_F = \pi_1 - x .
\end{align}
\begin{assumption}
$\Delta > 0$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}
There is one futures contract and it delivers bond 1.
\end{assumption}
Long bond 2 against the future earns
\begin{equation}
R_2 - R_F = (\pi_2 - \pi_1) + x - \Delta \varepsilon_S .
\label{eq:trade}
\end{equation}

\section{Pricing}

Asset managers have risk tolerance $A$ and carry factor exposures $e_L$ and $e_S$. Their first order conditions give
\begin{equation}
\pi_i = \ell \lambda_L + s_i \lambda_S , \qquad
\lambda_L = \frac{\sigma_L^2 e_L}{A}, \qquad
\lambda_S = \frac{\sigma_S^2 e_S}{A} ,
\end{equation}
with $s_1 = s$ and $s_2 = s + \Delta$. Hence
\begin{equation}
\pi_2 - \pi_1 = \Delta \lambda_S .
\label{eq:cheap}
\end{equation}
\begin{result}
Bond 2 is cheaper than bond 1 if and only if $\lambda_S > 0$. The cheapness is a risk premium.
\end{result}
Risk neutral arbitrageurs prefer bond 2 whenever $\lambda_S > 0$. Their demands are \eqref{eq:dh} and the dealer analogue with $x$ replaced by
\begin{equation}
x + \Delta \lambda_S .
\label{eq:comp}
\end{equation}
The basis trade is paid jointly for balance sheet and for slope risk.

\section{Equilibrium}

Supplies $S_1, S_2$, futures in zero net supply, arbitrageurs hold bond 2 against futures. Asset managers carry all level exposure, $\lambda_L = \sigma_L^2 \ell (S_1 + S_2)/A$, the term premium of the original. They carry the slope exposure the arbitrageurs do not,
\begin{equation}
\lambda_S = \frac{\sigma_S^2}{A} \left[ s S_1 + (s+\Delta) S_2 - \Delta (D_D + D_H) \right] .
\label{eq:lambdas}
\end{equation}
Together with futures clearing and the demand curves this is a linear system in $D_H, D_D, x, \lambda_S$.
\begin{result}
The compensation $x + \Delta \lambda_S$ is positive, falls with $E_H$ and $K_D$, and rises with $S_2$ and $\sigma_S^2$. Positions $D_H$ rise with $\sigma_S^2$.
\end{result}

\section{Fragility}

A realisation $\varepsilon_S > 0$ costs the hedge fund sector $L = \Delta D_H \varepsilon_S$ on a level hedged position. Through \eqref{eq:dh} the position it can carry falls by $L / \gamma$. As $D_H$ falls, $\lambda_S$ rises through \eqref{eq:lambdas} and $x$ rises through futures clearing, so bond 2 cheapens and funds that stay mark a further loss. This is the unwinding of the original with the capital shock generated by the factor the trade left unhedged. A rise in $\sigma_S^2$ alone has the same effect through $\lambda_S$.

\section{Sign check}

The model needs $\Delta > 0$ and $\lambda_S > 0$. The second is in the deck, the bonds funds hold have positive pricing errors. The first cannot come from the funds' futures positions, since EMIR identifies hedge funds only through their dealers, so it is measured from the bond side in analysis.do. Each US bond funds are long is paired with the cheapest to deliver of its contract, or the closest in duration if outside every basket. Two series follow, the duration of the held bond minus the CTD, and the net convexity of a dollar duration matched hedge, $C_B - (D_B/D_C) C_C$. Both are positive on every day with positions. The duration gap averages half a year and does not move with implied volatility. So funds hold the bond further out on the curve than the CTD, which is $\Delta > 0$ with the slope factor in its ordinary sense, and the exposure per unit of position is a stable feature of the bonds chosen rather than something funds time. The model's prediction on volatility concerns positions $D_H$, not $\Delta$, and is not tested here.

\section{Open points}

Only one futures contract, so the slope cannot be hedged. The fixed $\theta$ of the original. One period. $\sigma_S^2$ exogenous. The size of the exposure needs the hedge ratio, which the missing futures leg does not give.
